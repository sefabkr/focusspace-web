What is the Pomodoro Technique, and how do you use it?

The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method: you work on one task for 25 minutes, then take a 5-minute break, and after four rounds you take a longer break of 15–30 minutes. Each 25-minute round is a “pomodoro”. Francesco Cirillo created the method in the late 1980s and named it after his tomato-shaped kitchen timer: pomodoro is Italian for tomato.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Pick one task.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
  3. Work only on that task until the timer rings. If something else comes to mind, note it and go back to the task.
  4. Take a 5-minute break: stand up, drink water, look away from the screen.
  5. After four pomodoros, take a longer break of 15–30 minutes.
  6. Count your pomodoros. After a few days you know how many a task takes, and planning gets easier.

Questions

How long is a pomodoro?

25 minutes is the classic length, followed by a 5-minute break. Many people adjust it: 50 minutes with a 10-minute break for deep work, or 15 minutes on days when starting is hard. What matters is a fixed block and a real break.

What should I do during the break?

Something that isn't work: stand up, stretch, drink water, look away from the screen. Messages and social feeds tend to turn 5 minutes into 20.

What if I'm interrupted?

If it can wait, write it down and keep going. If it can't, stop the pomodoro and start a new one later: in the original method a pomodoro is either whole or it doesn't count.

Is the Pomodoro Technique good for studying?

Many students use it. Short blocks make it easier to start, and the breaks keep a long study day manageable. Plan your topics in pomodoros, for example “chapter 3: two pomodoros”, and look back at what you did at the end of the day.

Do I need a special timer?

Any timer works. A pomodoro app adds the breaks by itself, counts your rounds and shows the time left on the Lock Screen.
